The geometry of
absolute error.

Arnold Tao · June 2022 · Revisited in 2026

From brute force to a finite geometric search. In 2022, I independently explored least absolute deviations (LAD) regression and realized that the loss surface consists of flat facets. Instead of searching an entire grid, I could find a global optimum by testing the intersections of residual-zero lines.

Why it matters: no grid resolution or initial guess is needed. For two coefficients, the solution needs only basic geometry: lines, intersections, and the corners of polygons. LAD is established; the 2026 retrospective adds a proof and numerical validation.

Why is there a crease?

Keep the flow rate fixed. As the initial volume moves across the selected reading's zero-residual line, its signed error changes sign. Taking the absolute value folds the negative half upward. In two dimensions, that V becomes two planar faces joined along a crease.

The change of viewpoint

A line becomes a point. A reading becomes a line.

In the familiar data plot, a single pair of coefficients gives a whole predicted line. Turn the problem around: in coefficient space, that same pair is just one point. A reading, on the other hand, becomes the line of all coefficient pairs that fit it exactly:

Cross that line and the corresponding residual changes sign. Stay inside a region and every sign is fixed; the absolute values can be removed with the appropriate signs. The total error then becomes an affine function on that region.

That is the geometric shortcut. In the full-rank, unconstrained problem, at least one global minimum occurs at a corner where two independent residual-zero lines meet. Flat edges or faces can also contain minima, so this does not yet establish uniqueness. Instead of scanning an infinite continuum of coefficients, we can test a finite set of intersections.

04 / Elementary geometric proof

Why only the intersections?

Every measurement draws a straight line of zero error. Within any polygon between these lines, every residual has a fixed sign. Removing the absolute-value signs therefore leaves a single plane:

The geometric result. For two unknown coefficients, if at least two residual-zero lines are nonparallel, a global minimum occurs at an intersection of two nonparallel residual lines. No calculus, matrix inversion or optimization solver is needed.

1. A minimum cannot escape to infinity.

Take any trial point with total error M. A better point must have each individual absolute error at most M. For any one observation, that condition describes a strip between two parallel lines. Pick two observations whose lines are nonparallel: their strips intersect in a bounded parallelogram. All points as good as our trial lie inside it. The loss is continuous, so a minimum exists. Draw a large rectangle enclosing this entire parallelogram strictly inside it. No point on the outer rectangle can be optimal.

2. A sloping plane reaches its lowest height at a corner.

The residual lines cut that rectangle into convex polygons. On each polygon the loss is a plane. Draw parallel level lines across a polygon and slide them downhill: the last line touching the polygon must touch a corner or an entire edge. If it touches an edge, both endpoints have the same height. If the whole plane is flat, every corner has the same height. Either way, at least one corner is a lowest point.

3. Only real intersections matter.

Apply the argument to the polygon containing the global minimum. Its lowest corner cannot lie on the artificial outer rectangle. So it must be where two original, nonparallel residual-zero lines meet. That is the finite search I proposed in 2022. The rectangle is only used for the proof; the algorithm never needs to choose its size.

For the original five lines there are pairs. Find each crossing by ordinary substitution of one straight-line equation into the other, then compare the total absolute errors. Try the corner search ↑

The same geometric reasoning extends to more coefficients (hyperplanes and polyhedra), but the number of possible intersections grows rapidly. For two coefficients, testing all pairs and summing the errors takes cubic time in the number of observations; there is no grid resolution to refine.

06 / The geometry of uniqueness

Is the lowest corner unique?

A convex piecewise-planar surface can have a flat bottom, so the appearance of a single valley is not a proof. But the same corner argument settles this dataset.

Every point inside a polygon can be represented by a weighted average of its corners (for example, triangulate the polygon and use the three corners of the triangle containing the point). A plane has the same weighted-average property for height. If a point is at the global minimum, every corner with positive weight must also have that minimum height. Thus, if two different points minimized the loss, at least two different corners would have to share the minimum height. Corners on the artificial enclosing rectangle cannot minimize.

Evaluating the given mathematical model at high precision confirms this separation. Therefore the model has a unique minimum at lines 1 and 5. This is a property of these inputs, not a guarantee of LAD regression in general.

07 / Reflection

What I learned from the shape.

This is an independent exploration of an established estimator, least absolute deviations (LAD), or regression—not a claim to have invented it. Compared with least squares, it is often less sensitive to response outliers, though not immune to high-leverage predictor outliers. The geometry is the part of the project I found most compelling: a continuous, unbounded search can become a finite geometric problem.

The two exponential columns have a condition number near 183; small observation changes can cause much larger coefficient changes. The precision shown is computational, not evidence of physical measurement accuracy. The supplied data contain only five observations, so the original physical model and its extrapolations would require further validation.

Corrections made in the 2026 review

The early grid-search listing accidentally uses the same exponential rate twice, and takes the absolute value after summing errors, allowing cancellation. The later expression uses the intended two rates and sums absolute residuals. The geometry and validation on this page use the latter model. The original assertion that a plot proves uniqueness is replaced by the geometric argument and comparison of corner heights above.
