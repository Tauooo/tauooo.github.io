Making brain MRI
segmentation more reliable.

In Professor Zhi-Pei Liang’s group at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, I investigate computational approaches to white-matter segmentation. My focus is on understanding uncertainty in tissue boundaries, evaluating existing methods, and exploring how to make automated delineation more anatomically consistent.

The research question

How can we evaluate a predicted boundary when experts themselves disagree?

Brain MRI reveals complex anatomical structures, but translating an image into a reliable tissue map is not always straightforward. Even careful human annotations can differ at ambiguous boundaries. Understanding these differences is essential for deciding whether an automated segmentation is genuinely useful, rather than merely similar to one annotation.

01 / My contributions

From uncertainty to evaluation.

01

Studying expert disagreement

I work on comparing independent expert delineations of brain tissue to understand where annotations agree and where uncertainty arises. This helps frame the evaluation problem: a model should not be judged as though every manually drawn boundary were necessarily unambiguous.

02

Evaluating computational approaches

I investigate and compare established medical image segmentation and registration approaches, studying their assumptions and the ways their outputs can differ. I consider both quantitative agreement and whether the predicted tissue structures make anatomical sense.

03

Developing research directions

I review related literature, examine limitations of existing approaches, and formulate potential improvements and evaluation questions for the group’s ongoing work. I am particularly interested in how to reconcile local image evidence with broader anatomical consistency.

02 / Research perspective

Beyond a single accuracy score.

This experience has encouraged me to think of image analysis as an interplay between signal interpretation, computational modeling, and rigorous evaluation. A numerical improvement alone is not sufficient: it matters where an error occurs, how it changes a structure, and how confidently an apparent error can be identified.

Through this research, I am learning to translate open-ended imaging problems into testable questions, evaluate the strengths and limitations of published methods, and communicate the assumptions behind computational results. These questions are central to my interest in graduate research at the intersection of computing and medical imaging.
