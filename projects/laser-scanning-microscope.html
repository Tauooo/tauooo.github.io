04 / Personal build · January–February 2025

A microscope
from a Blu-ray pickup.

I repurposed a Blu-ray optical pickup into a laser scanning microscope, integrating its optics, precision motion, analog readout and microcontroller-based imaging. Instead of taking a photograph, the instrument builds an image one measurement at a time.

Videos on this site are silent for privacy.

Results: scanned a 10 µm-pitch calibration slide and styrofoam, and visualized the contours of onion cells in 3D. Two short original demo videos are immediately below.

What can it see?

Calibration, not a resolution claim

0.01 mm = 10 µm

The spacing between adjacent slide divisions is known, which makes the target useful for checking scan scale and spatial fidelity. Distinguishing the graduations is encouraging, but does not establish a 10 µm optical resolution. The raw sampling pitch, field of view and calibrated point-spread response are not available in this archive. No physical scale or calibrated height axis is assigned to the styrofoam or onion visualization.

Why reuse a Blu-ray pickup?

Instrument principle

One spot instead of a camera.

A disc-drive optical pickup already contains a tightly focused light source, objective optics, a photodetector and fine positioning mechanisms. Moving the illuminated spot relative to a specimen turns the reflected optical signal into a spatial image.

In the reference architecture, the objective voice-coil scans along one axis while a stepper-driven stage advances the other. The controller samples the photodiode signal at successive positions and the interface assembles those samples into a raster image.

My build documents the adapted pickup, breadboard electronics, stage, optical tests and reconstructed output. The underlying approach follows Doctor Volt’s open-source microscope; I distinguish the original design from my implementation below.

What I would characterize next.

From a working prototype to a measured instrument

Spatial calibration

Measure pixel distances over several known divisions in each scan axis to estimate µm per sample and test scan linearity and repeatability.

Optical resolution

Measure an edge response or a calibrated resolution target, rather than equating the graduation spacing with the optical resolution.

Signal and reconstruction

Quantify scan-direction artifacts, repeatability, photodetector noise and reconstruction drift. Establish a physical height calibration before assigning units to a surface plot.

Design provenance.
